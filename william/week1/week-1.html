Can machine learning help us spot wildfire sooner?

A quick guide to how wildfire detection works—and which data signals are worth testing in our B.C. project.

01 · DETECT

02 · DETECT EARLY

03 · FORECAST

Estimate future risk

Our project: We are forecasting near-term fire occurrence from historical fire and weather data. We are not building a live satellite alarm.

How machine learning helps detect fires

Satellites observe heat and light. Models look for unusual signals and patterns that change over time.

Look for heat

Thermal and shortwave-infrared images can reveal hot spots.

Look across time

GRU and other sequence models learn how a hotspot develops between images.

Combine information

Image models can combine satellite bands, weather, and landscape data.

Frequent satellite images can help with timeliness; finer-resolution images can show more spatial detail. Clouds, false alarms, and missing or imperfect fire labels remain challenges.

What the papers show

GRU + frequent imagery

Zhao and Ban use GOES-R time series to detect fires earlier in many study areas.

Predicting hotspot images

Saqer uses a self-attention GRU to predict future GOES hotspot images. Longer forecasts are harder.

Fuse data sources

CanadaFireSat combines Sentinel-2, MODIS, and weather data to forecast fire-labelled areas.

Each study answers a different question, so their performance scores are not directly comparable.

Features worth trying for our model

Start with a few understandable signals. Add more only if the data are reliable and improve validation results.

Weather today

Temperature, relative humidity, wind, and rainfall.

Why it matters

Heat and low humidity dry fine fuels; wind can help a fire grow; rain moistens fuels.

Value to the model

Gives the model current conditions that can make an ignition more likely to become a recorded fire.

Dryness over time

Rain totals over recent days, dry-spell length, and FWI components if available.

Why it matters

Fuels do not become wet or dry based only on today's weather; moisture reflects conditions over time.

Value to the model

Rolling rainfall and FWI features summarize accumulated dryness, helping capture delayed or combined weather effects.

Season and fuels

Day of year; vegetation or fuel type; terrain if available.

Why it matters

Fire conditions and fuel moisture change seasonally. Vegetation, slope, and elevation shape local fuel and fire behaviour.

Value to the model

Provides seasonal or location context, so similar weather can be interpreted differently across time and landscape.

Past fire activity

Recent fire counts, calculated only from records available before each prediction.

Why it matters

Ignition patterns can vary by place and time, reflecting factors such as lightning or human activity.

Value to the model

Adds historical ignition context that weather alone cannot provide. It must not include the fire being predicted or future records.

Why use this feature set? Together, the features represent weather, accumulated fuel dryness, seasonal and landscape context, and ignition history. They give a model complementary clues; none is a cause-proof or a guarantee that a fire will occur. FWI describes fire-conducive conditions, not the probability that a fire will start.

How these datasets guide EDA

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) means checking what the data contain, finding patterns and gaps, and deciding how to build fair model inputs. The notebook points to three useful sources:

NASA FIRMS · VIIRS hotspots

Fire observations with date/time, latitude/longitude, confidence, brightness, fire radiative power (FRP), and day/night.

ECCC · daily weather

Station observations including maximum temperature, total precipitation, minimum relative humidity, and maximum wind gust.

ECCC + DataBC · where

Station locations and dates, matched to B.C. fire-centre boundaries and hotspot locations.

Ask these questions before choosing features

  • Do hotspot counts change by year, month, or region? Plot counts over time and map the points. This helps choose a study region and spot a season or year that dominates the data.
  • Are detections repeated or uncertain? Compare confidence, day/night, brightness, and FRP; inspect detections close together in time and space. One FIRMS row is a hotspot observation, not necessarily one unique fire or its ignition date. This determines whether to filter, group, or count detections—and warns against labeling every row as a separate fire.
  • Do weather stations cover the same dates and places? For each station and summer, count expected versus available days, missing runs, and field flags; map station distance from hotspots. The notebook uses 459 expected May–September days per station across 2021–2023 and screens for at least 90% of days with both maximum temperature and precipitation. This helps select a region and stations, and tells us whether interpolation or a reduced study window is needed.
  • Do fire and weather records line up? Compare date ranges, time zones, station proximity, spatial resolution, and daily weather distributions. This guides the join and whether weather should be summarized by station, region, or grid cell.
  • Do the proposed signals differ on fire and non-fire dates? Plot temperature, humidity, rainfall, wind, and rolling dryness against the chosen fire label. This checks whether candidate features have useful patterns; it does not by itself prove causation or guarantee predictive value.
