# TabPFN for Wildfire Prediction: Literature Review

Published work on TabPFN, wildfire ML and early fire prediction.

**Summary:** Three published studies use TabPFN on wildfire problems. None use it for early detection or next-day ignition forecasting, which is our task. That looks like an open gap, and it's a point in our project's favour.

## 1. Studies that apply TabPFN to wildfire problems

### a) Fire spread type in Canadian conifer forests (Khanmohammadi, Cruz, Perrakis et al., ~2024)

This is the closest match to Canada. The study classifies Canadian conifer fires into three types: surface, passive crown and active crown. TabPFN beat the other ML methods in independent evaluations.

- Inputs were wind speed, fuel/stand variables and FFMC moisture content. TabPFN scored 91% accuracy and 93% F1 on the evaluation set, and clearly beat AutoGluon.
- **Caveat:** the evaluation set was tiny, n = 23. This shows TabPFN's main strength, which is small labelled datasets, but don't read the numbers as robust.

### b) Wildfire susceptibility mapping in Victoria, Australia, using AlphaEarth embeddings (UNSW, 2026)

This is the most rigorous recent benchmark that includes TabPFN.

- **Setup:** fire labels came from NASA FIRMS MODIS hotspots for 2017–2025, kept only at ≥80% confidence. Non-fire cells were sampled at a 1:1 ratio and placed at least 3 km away from fire cells. These are the same data sources we are using.
- **Results:** TabPFN-3 was the best cell-level model, with ROC-AUC 97.21% on physical variables. LightGBM reached 96.14% and XGBoost 95.80%. With embeddings as input, CNNs using 17×17 or larger neighbourhood patches beat TabPFN. With physical variables, the CNNs did not beat the best tabular models.
- **Limitations that matter for us:**
  - Training on a 1:1 sample means the scores show relative risk, not true ignition probabilities. Calibration would be needed to get real probabilities.
  - The authors say annual embeddings are not good standalone predictors for near-real-time or next-day forecasting. They should be combined with dynamic weather data.
  - The paper used random train/test splits, so the scores are probably optimistic.

### c) Post-wildfire debris-flow prediction, western US (arXiv, 2026)

TabPFN came first by AUC at 0.953, ahead of ExtraTrees (0.948), Random Forest (0.945), XGBoost (0.944) and CatBoost (0.939). The paper also checked SHAP explanations and found TabPFN's predictions followed feature relationships with the expected sign rather than spurious associations. One surprise: synthetic data generated by TabPFN helped other models but did not help TabPFN itself.

### Related: landslide susceptibility

The neighbouring field of landslide susceptibility also has a TabPFN-based framework that outputs a probability distribution over classes for each location, so it comes with uncertainty estimates built in.

## 2. TabPFN background to cite

- **TabPFN v2 (Hollmann et al., *Nature*, 2025)** is the main citation. It beat all previous methods on datasets of up to 10,000 samples. In 2.8 seconds it outperformed an ensemble of the strongest baselines tuned for 4 hours.
- **TabPFN-3 (2026)** matters because of scale. Version 2.5 handled 100,000 rows and 2,000 features, and version 3 goes to one million rows. It beats gradient-boosted trees tuned for 8 hours on datasets up to 1M rows and 200 features, and runs up to 20x faster than 2.5. A grid over BC with daily time steps will produce a lot of rows, so we'll likely need v3, or we'll need to subsample.
- **TabPFN-TS** is directly relevant because we're working with time-series data. It treats forecasting as a tabular regression problem and supports forecasts that use extra input variables. It reaches state-of-the-art results on covariate-informed forecasting. Since v1.1.0, the package uses the TabPFN-TS-3 checkpoint by default.
- **Drift-Resilient TabPFN (NeurIPS 2024 workshop)** is a variant that learns to handle changes in the data distribution over time. This could be useful because fire seasons change from year to year.

## 3. Early-prediction work in BC and Canada (no TabPFN) to use as baselines

- **BCWildfire dataset:** 25 years of daily data covering 240 million hectares of BC and nearby regions. It includes a benchmark of CNN, Transformer and Mamba models for next-day wildfire risk prediction. This is probably the best benchmark to compare against.
- **ECMWF Probability of Fire (PoF):** forecasts fire occurrence worldwide up to ten days ahead. It is an operational system we can compare with.
- **Laurentian University BC thesis:** combines CWFIS fire records with ERA5 weather data to predict fire occurrence in BC.
- **Canada next-day spread with a Video Swin U-Net (2026):** learns from three-day sequences, with wind and temperature sampled several times a day.

## 4. Where TabPFN fits in our project

| Our task | Evidence | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Static risk zones (susceptibility) | Strong: best tabular model in the Victoria study | No BC or boreal study yet |
| Early detection or next-day ignition from time series | None found using TabPFN | **Open gap, which is our possible contribution** |
| Fire behaviour after ignition | Canadian conifer study | Very small sample |

### Worked example: framing the task for TabPFN

Each row is one grid cell on one day. The features are:

- static terrain (elevation, slope, aspect)
- the day's weather from ERA5 or BCWS stations (temperature, relative humidity, wind)
- lagged features, such as FWI averaged over the last 3 and 7 days, consecutive dry days, and whether there were FIRMS hotspots within 10 km yesterday

The label is whether a FIRMS hotspot appears in that cell tomorrow. This is how TabPFN-TS handles time: it turns time into table columns.

The critical design choice is to **split by time, for example train on 2015–2022 and test on 2023**, rather than splitting randomly. A random split lets the model see days from the same fire event in both training and testing, which is the weakness of the Victoria study.

Then:

1. Run TabPFN-3 alongside LightGBM on the same split.
2. Report PR-AUC as well as ROC-AUC, because fire days are rare.
3. Calibrate the probabilities before they drive community alerts.

If TabPFN beats tuned boosted trees under a time-based split on BC data, that is a new and publishable result.
