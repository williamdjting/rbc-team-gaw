The problem

Will a satellite see fire in this cell tomorrow?

The forecast

  • BC is split into H3 resolution-7 cells (≈5 km² each): 234,554 cells across the six BC Wildfire Service Fire Centres.
  • For every cell, every day from May to September: will VIIRS record at least one hotspot there tomorrow (UTC day)?
  • Fires are rare: 0.135% of 2023 cell-days are positive.

The rules of the game

  • Train on 2018–2021, choose thresholds on 2022, evaluate on 2023.
  • 2024 is never loaded. It is kept for one final test.
  • Score: F1 per Fire Centre and BC-wide, with week-block bootstrap intervals for "model minus rule".

The goal

F1 of 0.70–0.80, with a machine-learning model clearly beating the persistence rule: "a cell that had fire yesterday will have fire today".

Diagnosis · end of October

Not a bug: the inputs hit an information limit

Before adding more models, we checked whether the problem itself capped the score.

0.49–0.66F1 of a cheating "lookup" model

Even a table fitted on 2023 itself, using the same history inputs, couldn't do much better.

≈0.55P(fire today | fire yesterday)

Half of "fire yesterday" cells show nothing today in the S-NPP label.

10–22%of days inside a fire run have no detection

A single satellite misses burning cells (cloud, smoke, scan gaps).

10–19%of positives come from fully quiet areas

No fire nearby in the last 7 days: invisible to any history input.

0.00–0.03F1 lost from 2022 thresholds

Thresholds transferring between years were not the problem.

Three possible ways forward: richer fire history, a fairer target, or better labels and new drivers. We tried them in that order.

Phase 3 · Experiment 14

Is the target too strict? Allowing a one-cell margin

Why: 40% of the rule's false alarms and 49% of its misses were a fire one cell away. At 5 km² cells, a detection one cell over is often the same fire.

With the margin, 5 of 6 centres reached 0.70. The goal range is reachable under a reasonable scoring definition.

Margin scoring: a forecast counts as correct if fire appears in the cell or one of its six neighbours, and a fire counts as caught if the cell or a neighbour was flagged.

A different use: a watch list (Experiment 18, partial run)

Rank quiet cells by risk each day and list the top 50 per Fire Centre. The models' lists contained 23–40% of 2023's quiet fires at some point in the week before they appeared, against 1–20% for simple rankings and ≈0.3% for a random list. Not a yes/no forecast, but useful to a duty officer.

Phase 4 · Experiment 19 · why new data

The label itself was noisy: one satellite misses burning fires

S-NPP passes over BC about twice a day. Cloud, smoke, the edge of the scan or a weak, smouldering fire means a burning cell is recorded as "no fire". A forecaster can then be marked wrong twice for one missed pass.

Day 1Day 2Day 3Day 4Day 5 Actually burningyesyesyesyesyes S-NPP label11011 New label11111

Day 3 in the S-NPP label: a correct "fire" forecast counts as a false alarm, and day 4's forecast (made from day 3's empty record) becomes a miss.

Fix 1: a second satellite

NOAA-20 carries the same VIIRS instrument in the same orbit, about 50 minutes behind S-NPP. A cell counts as burning if either satellite saw it.

Fix 2: fill single-day gaps

If a cell burned the day before and the day after, the day in between counts as burning. The persistence rule still only sees what was actually observed, so there's no look-ahead.

Is the rule a ceiling?

No: the rule is wrong about 86,000 times in 2023

Persistence would be a ceiling if fires only ever stopped on their last day. The data says otherwise.

110,244cells on fire yesterday: the rule's forecasts
117,828cells on fire today: the truth
71,106on fire on both days: correct forecasts
39,138 false alarms · 35.5%

Precision 71,106 / 110,244 = 0.645. The fire moved on, died down or was put out. Many cells burn for only a day or two.

46,722 misses · 39.7%

Recall 71,106 / 117,828 = 0.603. Fire spreading into a neighbouring cell (most of them) or fire appearing in a quiet area (9%; about half of those are non-wildfire burning).

The real ceiling is label noise and what is truly unpredictable. A model can beat the rule by predicting which way a fire spreads and which fires stop. Both depend on wind, slope, fuel, humidity and rain, which are inputs the models haven't had yet.

S-NPP or NOAA-20 gap-filled label, all six Fire Centres pooled, exact cell. F1 = 2 × 0.645 × 0.603 / (0.645 + 0.603) ≈ 0.624.

Why the models still lose

Same recall as the rule, lower precision

1 · Most models can't see NOAA-20

The 08 and stopgap models only have S-NPP history, so they can't even copy the two-satellite rule. They land next to the S-NPP rule (0.58).

2 · No inputs for spread or stopping

A burning cell has 6 neighbours. Without wind or slope, flagging them adds more false alarms than catches, which is exactly the precision drop.

3 · Little training fire

2019 and 2020 were near-empty seasons. The stopgap model's top input is day_of_year: it memorised when 2018 and 2021 peaked.

4 · Thresholds from a quiet year

2022 had ≈9,000 fire cells against ≈118,000 in 2023. A threshold tuned on 2022 is likely too low for 2023. The rule has no threshold to get wrong.

What would count as success

A model whose week-block bootstrap interval against the two-satellite rule is entirely above zero, gaining by catching spread (recall) without adding false alarms (precision).

Loose ends

  • Finish Experiment 18 (make it resumable so memory stops don't lose work).
  • Revisit the GNN once drivers exist: message passing should help most for spread.
  • Report the watch list as a separate product for quiet-area fires.
