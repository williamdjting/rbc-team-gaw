Experiment 34 · Fire starts

Can a model trained only on fire starts predict where the next fire will start?

Purpose: the next-day models mostly learn that a burning cell keeps burning, and they almost never flag a cell with no recent fire. This experiment keeps only the start of each fire, trains on those, and asks how well the models can say where the next one will appear.

6 Fire Centres · train 2018–2021 · thresholds 2022 · evaluate 2023 · 2024 excluded

Setup

  • Fire start: a cell positive on the label on day t (S-NPP or NOAA-20 from 2021, S-NPP before, one-day gaps filled) with no detection from the same source in the cell or its six neighbours on days t-7 to t-1. Only these quiet cell-days can start a fire, so every forecast is trained and scored on quiet cell-days only.
  • Fire-start models: CatBoost trained on quiet cell-days of 2018–2021 (all fire starts plus Experiment 21's 1% draw of the rest), with Experiment 21's inputs, then adding static layers, then day of year.
  • References: Experiment 21's next-day CatBoost (trained on all cell-days, loaded unchanged), two simple rankings and random lists.
  • Two ways to judge: F1 with a threshold tuned on 2022, as before; and a daily watch list of the top N quiet cells per Fire Centre, which suits rare events better than F1.

Conclusion

  • Fire starts are rare and mostly unpredictable a day ahead. 2023 had 5,250 fire starts, 9% of all 58,914 fire cell-days, about one in 6,444 quiet cell-days. The best BC-wide F1 is 0.060 on the exact cell and 0.090 with the margin, far from the 0.70–0.80 goal. Every model's precision is below 4%: for each correct flag there are more than 26 false alarms.
  • Training on fire starts alone does not beat the general next-day model. The fire-start model with Experiment 21's inputs minus Experiment 21's model, BC-wide F1: exact -0.018 (95% interval -0.023 to -0.009), margin +0.003 (95% interval -0.006 to +0.011). Median AP 0.0167 against 0.0195. Experiment 21's model already learned what it can about quiet cells from the same inputs.
  • Static layers and day of year make it worse. Median AP falls to 0.0089 with static layers and 0.0093 with day of year as well. With only 5,451 training starts, terrain and fuel likely let the model memorise where the training seasons' fires were.
  • As a daily watch list the models are useful. The top 50 quiet cells per Fire Centre per day, 0.07% to 0.31% of each centre's quiet cells, hold 12% of fire starts on the day (Experiment 21) or 11% (fire-start model), against 0.1% for a random list. Within the 7 days up to the start, 19% are listed at least once, against 0.8% at random.
  • What drives the ranking: weather (temperature, wind, drought codes) and the distance to an active BCWS wildfire. Lightning on the day before is no better than random, likely because lightning fires often smoulder for days before a satellite sees them. A fire within five rings in the previous 3 days alone catches 7% on the day.
  • Which starts are caught (top 50 within 7 days, fire-start model): older wildfires flaring up 22%, starts with no BCWS record 17%, new BCWS wildfires 13%.
  • 2022 is a weak threshold year for starts: it had only 680 fire starts against 5,250 in 2023, so the tuned thresholds rest on few cases.
  • Training uses S-NPP-only fire starts in 2018–2020; 2021–2023 also use NOAA-20, which finds more starts.
  • About half of quiet onsets have no BCWS record (Experiment 16): many are likely agricultural, slash or industrial burns, not wildfires.
  • 2023 has been used to compare many experiments; only 2024 will give an untouched number.
